# Pitfalls — Quality Check for Every Integral Analysis

Run through this list before finalizing any non-trivial analysis.

## Errors in the map

### 1. Quadrant absolutism
Treating one quadrant as the "real" one and the others as secondary.
- *Psychologism* (UL only): "It's all in your mind."
- *Scientism / reductionism* (UR only): "It's just brain chemistry."
- *Culturalism* (LL only): "It's all socially constructed."
- *Systems reductionism* (LR only): "Fix the incentives and everything follows."

**Check:** does every quadrant have real content, and did any quadrant get only one
generic bullet?

### 2. Flatland
Accepting only what can be measured from the outside (Right-Hand quadrants) as real.
Feelings, meaning and culture become "soft" factors. Common in business and tech.

**Check:** are the Left-Hand quadrants treated as causes, not just consequences?

### 3. The pre/trans fallacy
Pre-rational (before reason) and trans-rational (beyond reason, including reason)
are both non-rational, so they are easily confused.
- **Reductionism (ptf-1):** treating genuine trans-rational insight as childish
  regression (e.g. treating all mystical experience as infantile).
- **Elevationism (ptf-2):** romanticizing pre-rational impulses as higher wisdom
  (e.g. calling impulsive tantrums "authentic", or magical thinking "spirituality").

**Check:** does the analysis include reason, or simply bypass it?

### 4. State/stage confusion
Treating a temporary state as a permanent stage — a retreat high as transformation, a
burnout phase as low capacity.

### 5. Line confusion
Assuming high development in one line means high development in all. A brilliant
analyst is not automatically emotionally wise; a deep meditator is not automatically
ethical. *Spiritual bypassing* is using spiritual states to avoid shadow, emotions and
relationships.

### 6. Level and type confusion
Calling a type difference a level difference ("they are less developed") when it is
really style, temperament or culture.

## Errors in tone and ethics

### 7. Level elevationism and labeling
Using the color spectrum to rank the worth of people, or to dismiss them ("that's so
Amber"). Higher stages are more inclusive, not more valuable as people. Every level is
needed for a healthy spiral.

### 8. "Second-tier" arrogance
Believing that knowing the map makes you integral. Knowing about stages is cognitive
(and can happen at any level); *being* able to act from them is a different thing.

### 9. Mean green
Attacking all hierarchy, including healthy ones (competence, growth hierarchies), in the
name of equality.

### 10. Cultural bias
Assuming that the developmental sequence looks the same in every culture, or that one
culture's center of gravity is "behind". Describe structures, not nations.

## Errors in practice

### 11. False precision
Presenting estimates ("this team is 62% Orange") as measurement. Use words like
*seems*, *appears*, *likely*. Offer ways to test the hypothesis (see
[eight-zones.md](eight-zones.md)).

### 12. Jargon overload
Burying a simple insight under AQAL vocabulary. If the user did not ask for theory,
lead with plain language and use terms sparingly.

### 13. Map without action
A complete analysis with no next steps. Every non-Learn output should end with
concrete actions.

### 14. Forcing symmetry
Inventing content for a quadrant to make the table look complete. If a quadrant
genuinely carries little weight here, say so in one line.

### 15. Ignoring the user's own quadrants
Analyzing "them" but not the person asking. The user's own interior (UL), behavior
(UR), relationships (LL) and context (LR) are part of the situation.

## Honest limits of the model

- Integral Theory is a philosophical *synthesis*. Parts of it rest on well-studied
  research (developmental psychology, systems theory, contemplative science); other
  parts are interpretive or speculative.
- Spiral Dynamics and some stage models are popular but have limited independent
  empirical validation; ego-development research (Loevinger, Cook-Greuter) has stronger
  measurement traditions.
- Critics note that the model can be applied in an elitist way, that it can
  over-schematize, and that Wilber's readings of some traditions are contested.
- Use the model as a **completeness checklist and a source of good questions**, not as a
  final authority. When stakes are high — health, mental health, law, finance, safety —
  recommend qualified professionals.
